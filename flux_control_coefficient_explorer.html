Try it yourself

1. The "limiting" enzyme. Set E1 low and E2 high (try u₁=0.2, u₂=8). What happens to CE1J? Which enzyme is effectively "in control" of the flux here, and why does that match the idea of a rate-limiting step?

2. Shared control. Set u₁=u₂=1. What are CE1J and CE2J now? Explain in words what it means for control to be "distributed equally" between two enzymes.

3. Diminishing returns. Starting from u₁=u₂=1, slide u₁ up toward 10 while leaving u₂ fixed. Watch the linear plot: why does the curve flatten out, and what does a flattening slope tell you about CE1J as an enzyme becomes more abundant relative to its partner?

4. The Summation Theorem. Move both sliders to several different combinations and watch the C1+C2 value and the stacked bar. Does the sum ever change? Using the equation J = u₁u₂/(u₁+u₂), can you see algebraically why CE1J + CE2J must equal 1?

5. Reading the ln–ln plot. Switch to the ln–ln view. The text states that CEiJ equals the slope of ln J vs ln ui. Confirm this: does the tangent slope shown on the log–log plot match the CE1J value in the stat panel?

6. Connecting to GAPDH. The Shestov et al. data showed that hexokinase and PFK, both thermodynamically "favored," had near-zero or negative flux control coefficients, while GAPDH's was strongly positive. Using this two-enzyme model as an analogy, what would u1 vs u2 need to look like for E1 to behave like GAPDH — the step that truly controls the flux — even though it isn't the most thermodynamically favorable step?
